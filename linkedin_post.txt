Taken at face value, the official numbers describe a labor market close to balance. Unemployment averaged 4.28% over the first ten months of 2026, by this project's count from Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Population Survey microdata, weighted to represent the full working-age population of the United States. That matches the average of the BLS's own published, not seasonally adjusted Table A-15 figures for January through October to the hundredth of a percentage point.

The broader measures say more. Add the people who want a job, could start one, and looked for work in the past year but not the past four weeks, along with those working part time because full-time hours aren't available, and the rate rises to 7.95%. The BLS publishes 7.96%. An earlier version of this analysis came in at 7.44% and described the 0.66-point gap as a difference in definitions. It was an error: the filter for marginally attached workers left out everyone who had searched for work without holding a job in the past year. Switching to the Census Bureau's own classification for that group closed the gap, and U-4 and U-5 now match the published figures as well.

The part-time count has a size to it. About 4.7 million people a month worked part time for economic reasons, on average 12.3 hours a week short of full time. That adds up to roughly 57.6 million hours of work people wanted and couldn't get, every week.

Then there is overqualification. About one in seven employed Americans (14.95%) has noticeably more schooling than is typical for their occupation, measured with the realized-matches method. For every occupation with at least 30 survey respondents, the analysis converts education to years of schooling and computes the weighted national average and spread; anyone more than one standard deviation above their occupation's average is counted. The method needs no outside credential database or O*NET crosswalk, only the data on who is working where and how much schooling they bring to it.

The data can't name a single cause, and claiming one would go beyond the evidence. What it does show is a consistent distance between the headline rate and the broader measures, built into how the official count defines work. Labor market analysis that means to serve the people it describes should start from measures that hold more of their experience, and should say plainly where each measure stops.
